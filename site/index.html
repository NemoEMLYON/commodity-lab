Beyond the material

Fifteen years of gold, crude oil, copper, natural gas and corn. Can machine learning, fed with geopolitics, inventories and weather, forecast next month's volatility better than a simple regression?

Not on average. Crude oil is the exception: with the volatility priced by its options market, the forecast loss falls by about 20% against the HAR benchmark.

The five materials

Each ETF holds futures on one commodity. Pick one to see fifteen years of its risk, measured as the annualised volatility of daily returns over 22 trading days.

01

Risk moves

A manager who holds a commodity ETF and wants a steady 10% annual risk has one lever: how much of it to hold. Hold less when volatility is about to rise, more when it is about to fall.

Doing nothing leaves the risk wherever the market puts it. Steering it with a forecast of next month's volatility brings it back near the target, for every commodity. The whole question is how good that forecast can be.

02

Only what was known

Every input enters the model on the day it was public, not on the day it describes. Inventories measured in the week to Friday come out the next Wednesday. The geopolitical risk index is refreshed once a week.

Use a number a few days early and any model looks brilliant in a backtest. These dates are enforced in the R code and checked by automated tests.

03

A 2009 regression against machine learning

The benchmark is HAR (Corsi, 2009): next month's volatility explained by the volatility of the last day, week and month. Three numbers, one regression.

Against it: a Lasso and a random forest, each trained twice, once on the commodity's own returns and once with implied volatility, the dollar, inventories, geopolitical risk and weather. Forecast loss is measured with QLIKE, out of sample, from 2017.

04

Where the edge is

Adding one block of inputs at a time shows what each one brings. Implied volatility is the block that helps, where a commodity has its own options index: OVX for crude oil, GVZ for gold.

Crude oil with implied volatility is the one result that holds up: about 20% lower loss than HAR, significant at the 1% level, and better in every test year except 2024. Inventories and weather add nothing prices did not already know.

Method and limits

Everything on this page was computed in R and exported as data files; the site only draws them. The code, the tests and the data pipeline are public.

Data and sources
  • ETF prices (GLD, USO, CPER, UNG, CORN and UUP for the dollar): daily adjusted closes from Yahoo Finance via quantmod, January 2011 to October 2026.
  • Implied volatility: VIX, OVX and GVZ, Cboe indices from FRED.
  • Inventories: EIA API v2, weekly US commercial crude stocks (WCESTUS1) and Lower 48 working gas in storage (NW2_EPG0_SWO_R48_BCF).
  • Geopolitical risk: daily GPR index, Caldara and Iacoviello (2022), American Economic Review 112(4), CC BY.
  • Weather: daily mean temperature for ten US cities, Open-Meteo historical archive (ERA5 reanalysis), CC BY 4.0. Weather data by Open-Meteo.com.
  • Data quality: on days with zero volume the previous close is kept, since nothing traded. This removes one bad print in CPER on 4 December 2014.
Target

The log of annualised realised volatility over the next 21 trading days: √(252 × mean of squared daily log returns), from closing prices, floored at 1% before the log. Close-to-close returns are the ones a holder of the ETF actually bears.

No look-ahead

Each input carries the date it became usable: ETF closes the same day; implied volatility and weather the next trading day; EIA inventories the trading day after publication (Wednesday for crude, Thursday for gas); the geopolitical index the Wednesday after its Monday update. Values are attached to the trading calendar with an as-of join.

Automated tests check the dates, perturb single returns, and rebuild the whole table from only the data published at a cutoff date. Four deliberately injected leaks were all detected.

Models

Naive (last 22 days), EWMA (λ = 0.94), HAR estimated by OLS (Corsi, 2009), Lasso (glmnet, penalty chosen by purged blocked cross-validation inside each training window, one-standard-error rule) and random forest (ranger, 500 trees, per asset, minimum leaf size 200, chosen on 2015–2016 only). Inputs are added in blocks: own returns, implied volatility, geopolitical risk, then inventories, weather and the dollar where relevant.

Validation and tests

Walk-forward from 2017: each year, models are refitted on all earlier data whose target was already known (21-day embargo) and forecast the whole year. Primary loss: QLIKE on variance, with a lognormal (Jensen) correction when converting log forecasts. Diebold-Mariano tests use Newey-West errors with 21 lags; Mincer-Zarnowitz regressions check calibration. The main comparison (best model against HAR, five commodities pooled) was fixed in advance. About 65 model-by-commodity tests were run, so an isolated p-value below 5% is treated with caution.

Trading rules

Weight = target volatility / forecast volatility, capped at 2; rebalanced weekly when the weight moves by more than 10%; 5 basis points per unit traded; idle cash earns 0% and leverage is free. The portfolio version weights the five ETFs by inverse forecast volatility and scales them to the target with a trailing one-year correlation matrix.

Limitations
  • Overlapping 21-day targets leave about 120 independent observations per commodity out of sample: tests have limited power.
  • The geopolitical index, weather reanalysis and EIA gas storage are revised after the fact; real-time vintages were not available.
  • CPER traded thinly in 2012–2016; USO changed its futures roll in 2020.
  • The backtest ignores financing costs, taxes and market impact. Past results do not predict future ones. Not investment advice.
Reproduce

With R 4.6 in the project folder: renv::restore(), then source("pipeline.R"), source("robustness.R") and source("export_site.R"). Tests: testthat::test_dir("tests") in R and node site/tests/test_simulator.js for the simulator.
